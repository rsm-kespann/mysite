A Replication of Karlan and List (2007)

Author

Kennedy Spann

Published

June 11, 2025

Introduction

Dean Karlan at Yale and John List at the University of Chicago conducted a field experiment to test the effectiveness of different fundraising letters. They sent out 50,000 fundraising letters to potential donors, randomly assigning each letter to one of three treatments: a standard letter, a matching grant letter, or a challenge grant letter. They published the results of this experiment in the American Economic Review in 2007. The article and supporting data are available from the AEA website and from Innovations for Poverty Action as part of Harvard’s Dataverse.

Description of the experiment…

To ensure the results would be broadly applicable and allow for analysis of heterogeneous treatment effects, the experiment was conducted using a communication channel commonly used by large charities: direct mail solicitations. This setup allowed the authors to study donor behavior in a real-world fundraising context, rather than in a laboratory or hypothetical setting.

Participants were randomly assigned into two main groups:

  • A treatment group (67% of the sample), which received a fundraising letter that included an offer of a matching grant, and
  • A control group (33% of the sample), which received a similar letter without any mention of a match.

Only the treatment group was further subdivided into sub-treatments, varying along three key dimensions:

  • Match Ratio — how much the lead donor would match each dollar donated:
    • 1:1, 2:1, or 3:1
    • Lower effective prices should increase demand/giving.
  • Maximum Size of the Matching Grant — the total cap on matching contributions:
    • $25,000, $50,000, $100,000, or left unspecified
    • Economic intuition: a higher cap might signal seriousness or urgency, making the offer more compelling.
  • Example Donation Amount — the amount shown in the letter to illustrate the impact of a gift:
    • Based on each donor’s past giving behavior: either equal to, 1.25×, or 1.5× their highest previous contribution
    • One key finding: matching offers were significantly more effective in red states, despite the organization being liberal. This suggests that context, identity, and political environment can mediate treatment effectiveness.

By independently randomizing these three features across participants in the treatment group, the study could test not only whether matching gifts increase donations, but also which features of matching offers—like generosity or framing—are most influential.

This project seeks to replicate their results.

Balance Test

As an ad hoc test of the randomization mechanism, I provide a series of tests that compare aspects of the treatment and control groups to assess whether they are statistically significantly different from one another.

Treatment group: 33,396 observations
Control group: 16,687 observations

Variable Testing:

Below are balance tests on several pre-treatment variables:

  • mrm2 (months since last donation) - regression also included
  • freq (number of prior donations)
  • hpa (highest previous donation amount).

In each case, the t-test showed no statistically significant difference between treatment and control groups (p-values all > 0.05), indicating that these groups were well balanced on prior donation behavior.

This aligns with the purpose of Table 1 in Karlan and List (2007), which presents a range of descriptive statistics for treatment and control groups to show that randomization created comparable samples. Reproducing this kind of analysis is essential in confirming that any differences observed in later outcomes can be confidently attributed to the experimental treatment, not to pre-existing differences between the groups.

Experimental Results

Charitable Contribution Made

First, I analyzed whether matched donations lead to an increased response rate of making a donation.

A statistical comparison using a t-test showed that this difference is statistically significant, with a p-value of 0.013:

Statistic    Value
-------------------------
t-statistic  3.2095
p-value      0.0013

In plain terms, this means that the difference in donation behavior (larger in the treatment group) is unlikely to be due to chance.

Even though the financial difference is small, simply framing a donation opportunity with a matching offer significantly increased the likelihood that people would give. This supports the idea that donors respond not just to financial incentives, but to social framing and perceived impact. When people feel their donation is part of something larger — like a matching challenge — they may be more likely to act.
